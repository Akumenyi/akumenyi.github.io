*The views expressed here are the author's own and do not represent positions of the
CVF-V20 or its member governments.*

In July 2025, African environment ministers meeting in Nairobi
[reaffirmed their "unequivocal rejection"](https://srm360.org/news-reaction/african-ministers-reject-srm/)
of stratospheric aerosol injection and called for an international non-use agreement.
Yet the largest
[cross-national surveys of climate intervention attitudes](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-46341-5)
show something awkward for that position. Publics in developing countries express more
support for researching climate interventions, including solar radiation modification
(SRM), than publics in the wealthy countries where most of the research happens. The same
respondents also worry more that SRM could undermine mitigation and distribute risks
unequally between poor and rich countries.

That is not confusion but rather one may ask what a risk-risk problem looks like from the
front line of climate change.

A recent SRM360 Perspective by Mark Borsuk, Tyler Felgenhauer and Jonathan Wiener
[defended risk-risk analysis](https://srm360.org/perspective/risk-risk-analysis-moving-beyond-misunderstanding/)
against its critics, arguing that comparing the risks of SRM against the risks of climate
change broadens rather than narrows the policy conversation. I want to push that argument
one step further. The countries that need this comparison most are the more than seventy
climate vulnerable economies of the Climate Vulnerable Forum and the V20, and they are
currently the least equipped to run it on their own terms. For them, refusing to weigh
SRM's risks against climate risks is not caution. It is a decision, and it has a ledger of
its own.

## The ledger is already unequal

Risk-risk analysis starts from a simple premise. Every option changes the portfolio of
risks, including the option of doing nothing new. For climate vulnerable countries the
portfolio without SRM is not a neutral baseline. It is the steepest downside in the
distribution.

The economics are documented. Diffenbaugh and Burke
[estimate](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1816020116) that anthropogenic warming
has already reduced per capita GDP by 17 to 31 percent across the poorest four deciles of
countries, leaving the gap between the richest and poorest deciles about 25 percent larger
than it would be in a world without warming. That damage accrued before the world had even
reached 1.2 degrees of warming. The V20 economies are now planning around trajectories
that pass 1.5 degrees, with overshoot increasingly treated as a planning assumption rather
than a tail risk.

This asymmetry is what makes abstention expensive. When a Norwegian or German policymaker
declines to engage with SRM evidence, the risk they accept on behalf of their citizens is
real but bounded. When the same posture is adopted on behalf of Bangladesh, Ghana or
Tuvalu, the accepted risk is of a different order. Whatever one concludes about SRM, the
conclusion should be reached by weighing, not by refusing to weigh.

## The evidence cuts both ways, and it cuts through us

Here is the uncomfortable part for both camps in this debate. The peer-reviewed evidence
on SRM's regional effects concentrates both the largest projected benefits and the most
serious projected harms in climate vulnerable countries.

On the benefit side, climate econometric work by Harding and colleagues
[finds](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-13957-x) that across all model
specifications tested, inter-country income inequality is lower with solar geoengineering
than without it, because cooling disproportionately benefits hot tropical economies. Their
more recent [mortality analysis](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2401801121)
estimates that a degree of cooling in a 2.5 degree world would avoid over 400,000
temperature-attributable deaths annually, concentrated in hotter and poorer regions, with
a central estimate that cooling benefits outweigh the direct mortality risks of sulfate
aerosols roughly thirteenfold. The confidence intervals are wide, the probability that
benefits exceed those direct risks at all is put at 61 percent and the authors say plainly
that this is not a comprehensive risk-risk evaluation. But the sign and the geography of
the central estimates matter.

On the harm side, the same geography appears with the sign reversed. Carlson and colleagues
[show](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-022-29613-w) that in a high-emissions
scenario, geoengineered cooling could nullify a projected reduction of almost one billion
people at highest malaria risk by 2070, with West Africa alone seeing on the order of 200
million more people in stable transmission zones. My own work with colleagues
[finds](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/2752-5295/adee3c) that in the
ARISE-SAI-1.5 simulations, regions of West and Central Africa that would otherwise see
intensifying rainfall extremes instead see reductions in both frequency and intensity,
while some drier regions gain rainfall, with strong heterogeneity across indices and
subregions. A [GeoMIP analysis](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2023EF003779)
led from the region finds weakened West African monsoon rainfall under sulfate injection,
with the sharpest declines in the Sahel. As a recent SRM360 Perspective on
[Tanzania](https://srm360.org/perspective/lessons-on-srm-from-tanzania/) illustrated,
cooling that eases heat stress can arrive together with drying that threatens rainfed
agriculture, which employs the majority of the working population in much of Africa.

None of this settles anything, and that is precisely the point. If the largest stakes on
both sides of the ledger sit in the same set of countries, then aggregate global analysis
is the wrong instrument and abstention is the wrong response. The only defensible path is
comparative assessment resolved at the scale where vulnerable people actually live, done
with the participation of the countries concerned.

## Three conditions for a risk-risk analysis we can trust

Critics of risk-risk framing are not wrong about everything. Duncan McLaren
[argues](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/european-journal-of-risk-regulation/article/reconstructing-riskrisk-analysis-to-support-effective-governance-of-highrisk-climate-interventions/29387025F77D041201A7EC87D06926CB)
that existing SRM risk comparisons tend to pair an idealized, well-governed deployment
against an unconstrained warming baseline, underweight sociopolitical risks and treat
distribution as a footnote. From where I sit, these are not reasons to abandon the
framework. They are the specification for doing it properly. Three conditions follow.

First, realistic baselines on both sides. The climate scenario should reflect the warming
trajectories vulnerable countries actually face, including overshoot. The SRM scenario
should include non-ideal deployment, contested governance and the termination problem, not
only the well-behaved simulations. A comparison that idealizes either side is advocacy
wearing the costume of analysis.

Second, distribution before aggregation. A global mean benefit is compatible with
concentrated regional harm, and the reverse. The
[Felgenhauer and colleagues framework](https://academic.oup.com/oocc/article/5/1/kgaf012/8089845)
explicitly calls for cataloguing who bears each risk. That element should be treated as the
core of the exercise rather than a sensitivity test. Vulnerability-weighted metrics,
subregional resolution and sectors that matter to the V20, including rainfed agriculture,
fisheries, hydropower and vector-borne disease, belong in the main table, not the appendix.

Third, the weighing must be done, in substantial part, by the countries with the most at
stake. Rahman, Artaxo, Asrat and Parker
[made this argument in 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-018-03917-8). People in
the Global South are on the front line of climate change and should lead the research
agenda that concerns them most. Programs like the [Degrees Initiative](https://www.degrees.ngo/)
have since supported SRM research teams across the developing world, including several of
the African studies cited above. That capacity remains a fraction of what sits in a handful
of Northern institutions. If risk-risk analysis is to carry legitimacy in Accra, Dhaka or
Suva, vulnerable-country scientists cannot be data points and reviewers. They must be
authors, and their governments must be able to commission assessments they did not
outsource.

## Abstention is also a position

The current international politics of SRM run in the opposite direction. The
[AMCEN rejection](https://srm360.org/news-reaction/african-ministers-reject-srm/), the
[EU Council's call for a deployment moratorium](https://www.ciel.org/solar-geoengineering-developments-spring-2026/)
and the collapse of successive resolutions at the UN Environment Assembly all reflect a
reasonable instinct. Countries that did not cause this crisis do not want a Northern-built
technology managed over their heads. I share that instinct. Nothing here is an argument for
deployment, and the sociopolitical risks of SRM, from mitigation deterrence to geopolitical
conflict, deserve the same rigor as the biophysical ones.

But an instinct is not an assessment. A non-use posture adopted before the comparative
analysis is done locks in the risks of the no-SRM pathway just as surely as premature
deployment would lock in the risks of intervention. Those baseline risks fall most heavily
on the very countries urged to look away. The
[UNEP working paper published this May](https://wedocs.unep.org/handle/20.500.11822/49509)
concluded that current evidence does not support SRM as a viable climate response while
still calling for expanded research. Insufficient evidence for deployment is not evidence
against viability. It is a mandate for exactly the kind of structured, distributionally
honest, vulnerable-country-led comparison that risk-risk analysis, done to the three
conditions above, is designed to deliver.

The Climate Vulnerable Forum's member states did not choose to live on the steep part of
the damage function. They can still choose to hold the pen when the risks are weighed. The
alternative is that the weighing happens anyway, elsewhere, about us and without us.
