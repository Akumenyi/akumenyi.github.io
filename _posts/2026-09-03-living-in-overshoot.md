UNEP said the quiet part out loud this week in a new report, Limiting Overshoot, the first UN assessment to state plainly that the world will cross 1.5&#176;C of warming within the next few years, not that it might. The question the report takes up is no longer whether we breach the Paris Agreement's safer limit but how far above it we go, how long we stay there and what it takes to come back down.

Those numbers deserve a moment of attention, starting with the fact that warming currently stands at about 1.4&#176;C above pre-industrial levels. The most optimistic pathway UNEP can construct peaks near 1.8&#176;C around mid century before long-term carbon removal brings temperatures back below 1.5&#176;C by 2100. Current policies point instead to roughly 2.6&#176;C by the end of the century. The report also puts a price on hesitation, since every five years of delay in cutting emissions adds about a tenth of a degree to the eventual peak.

Scientists reacting to the report have called it a reality check, which it is. Andy Reisinger noted it is the first report by a UN agency to say unambiguously that 1.5&#176;C will be crossed. Others cautioned that even the 1.8&#176;C best case is very optimistic. But for those of us working with climate-vulnerable economies, the report is less a revelation than a formal acknowledgment of what our member countries have been living through and warning about for years.

## Overshoot is not a detour

The language of overshoot can make exceedance sound like a temporary excursion, a brief trip above the line before normal service resumes. That framing is dangerously misleading, because overshoot is a period in which coral reefs, coastlines and lives are lost, some of them irreversibly. Reefs that die at 1.7&#176;C do not regrow because the thermometer later reads 1.4&#176;C. Aquifers that turn saline stay saline. People displaced from low-lying coasts do not un-migrate. The report itself is clear that above 1.5&#176;C the risk of triggering Earth system tipping points rises with every fraction of a degree. Some of those thresholds have no natural reversal.

We are watching this play out in real time, most recently on August 22, when the daily global average sea surface temperature reached 21.1&#176;C and broke the record set in March 2024. That should give everyone pause, because the global ocean normally peaks in March or April, which makes an August record far out of season. It is happening as a very strong El Niño develops in the Pacific, with forecasters now giving better than 90 percent odds of a very strong event this winter and real odds of one of historic proportions. El Niño is adding heat to the system, but it is doing so on top of decades of accumulated human-driven warming. This is what the front edge of overshoot looks like, landing hardest on the countries that contributed least to causing it.

## Carbon removal just moved to the center of the table

The most consequential shift in the UNEP report is structural, because once you accept that we will exceed 1.5&#176;C, returning below it stops being optional ambition and becomes the plan. The only route back then runs through net-negative emissions, meaning carbon dioxide removal at a scale far beyond anything deployed today, sustained for decades and layered on top of, never in place of, deep and immediate emissions cuts.

That includes the ocean, where marine carbon dioxide removal, from ocean alkalinity enhancement to approaches that work through marine ecosystems, is moving from the margins of the research literature toward the center of climate strategy. The ocean is the largest active carbon sink on the planet as well as the foundation of food security, livelihoods and culture for most climate-vulnerable economies, which means any serious overshoot strategy will eventually knock on their door.

Which is exactly why governance cannot be an afterthought. UNEP itself stresses that removal must be carefully governed, and that justice and equity must underpin the entire response. The historical pattern gives little comfort, since vulnerable countries have too often been presented with rules written elsewhere and invited to comply. If marine carbon removal follows that pattern, it will fail twice over, first as science, because the ocean observations and ecological baselines these approaches need sit largely in the waters of developing countries, and second as politics, because no durable social license will exist for interventions imposed on the communities that host them.

## Writing the rules, not inheriting them

At the CVF-V20 we are developing a framework for how climate-vulnerable countries engage marine carbon dioxide removal on their own terms. The premise is simple, namely that the countries with the most at stake in returning below 1.5&#176;C should help write the rules for the tools that get us there.

In practice that means ocean pathways assessed against the science rather than the hype, with research standards and monitoring that vulnerable-country institutions help design and run. It means biodiversity safeguards built in from the start, consistent with the commitments countries have already made on ocean biodiversity. It means the interests of coastal communities treated as a design constraint, not a consultation box to tick after the fact. It also means being honest in both directions, because some pathways may prove safe, effective and beneficial to host countries while others may not survive scrutiny. A credible framework has to be able to say no as well as yes.

None of this softens the first-order demand, which is that the fastest, cheapest and safest way to limit overshoot remains cutting emissions now, with the greatest responsibility sitting on the largest emitters. Carbon removal governed well is a complement to that work. Carbon removal used as an excuse to delay it would guarantee the worst outcomes the UNEP report warns about.

## Every tenth of a degree

The report's most useful contribution may be its arithmetic of delay. A tenth of a degree sounds like nothing until you notice that for a climate-vulnerable economy it is measured in bleached reefs, salinized fields, storm losses and debt taken on to rebuild what the last storm took. Peak warming of 1.7&#176;C instead of 1.8&#176;C is not a rounding error but a difference measured in livelihoods.

So yes, the reality check is welcome, though we should be equally clear about what it obliges. Deeper cuts now, adaptation and loss and damage finance treated as the core of the response rather than negotiable extras, and removal governance that vulnerable countries help write rather than inherit. The world has conceded that we are going to overshoot. What remains within our control is by how much, for how long and who gets a say in the way back.
