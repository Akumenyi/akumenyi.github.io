The Copernicus Climate Change Service released its October bulletin this morning, and the number that matters most is not the monthly record, though there is one of those too. The monthly average sea surface temperature in the Niño 3.4 region reached 29.94&#176;C, an all-time high for any month in the record and 3.21&#176;C above average, beyond the peaks of the 1982-83, 1997-98 and 2015-16 events that defined what a very strong El Niño looks like. October itself was the warmest October ever measured, at 1.75&#176;C above the pre-industrial level, the third month in a row above 1.5&#176;C. The ocean surface outside the poles averaged 20.95&#176;C, a record for the month.

The number that matters most sits behind those, because Copernicus now expects the average global temperature for 2023 through 2026 to finish above 1.5&#176;C. A single hot year can be argued away as El Niño noise, which is roughly what happened after 2024, the first calendar year above the threshold. A four-year average cannot. The Paris limit was always understood as a long-term level rather than a single-year spike, and the long-term measures are now arriving at the line one after another. 2026 is virtually certain to finish above 1.5&#176;C, with Copernicus putting the full year between 1.56&#176;C and 1.61&#176;C, an 80 percent chance of second place behind 2024 and a 20 percent chance of a new record. Because global temperature lags the Pacific peak by a few months, forecasters expect 2027 to run hotter still.

## A fiscal event, not an abstraction

For climate-vulnerable economies this is not an abstraction about thresholds. A very strong El Niño moves harvests, hydropower, food prices and disaster spending in the same budget year, and it does so in countries already stretched by debt. The V20's latest Debt Review counts 25 member economies spending between 10 and 20 percent of government revenue on debt service and another eight spending more than 20 percent. The last very strong event, in 2015-16, left droughts across southern Africa and Central America and fiscal scars that outlasted the Pacific anomaly by years. This one is arriving warmer at the base, on an ocean that has set surface temperature records for most of the past four years.

COP31 opens in Antalya on Monday against exactly this backdrop, and the early signals on finance are not encouraging. The EU is [reported to be arriving without new finance pledges](https://www.climatechangenews.com/2026/11/05/cop31-briefing-what-to-expect-from-the-antalya-climate-summit/). Developing countries are still fighting to get a number attached to the goal of tripling adaptation finance by 2035, a goal that would cover [a fifth to at most a third of assessed needs](https://www.unep.org/resources/adaptation-gap-report) even if it were met. The loss and damage fund made its [first allocation last month](https://www.climatechangenews.com/?p=91530), US$20 million set aside for Nepal 52 days after its request, against first-round requests across the fund reported at US$2.8 billion. The machinery that is supposed to absorb shocks like this El Niño is being argued over at the exact moment the shock is arriving.

## What taking the bulletin seriously would mean

In Antalya it would mean treating the four-year average as the headline it is, writing the overshoot plainly into the cover decision rather than softening it, and matching that honesty with delivery. Adaptation finance with a number and a pathway. Rapid-disbursement windows in the loss and damage fund, so that the next Nepal does not wait 52 days for an allocation and longer still for cash. Anticipatory finance keyed to forecasts, because an El Niño is the most forecastable large shock the climate system produces, and this one has been visible in the models since spring.

The Pacific has given negotiators the clearest possible reading of where the system stands. Three months above 1.5&#176;C, four years above it on average, and the strongest El Niño signal ever measured in the region that sets the world's weather. The question for the next two weeks is whether the response moves at the speed of the water.
